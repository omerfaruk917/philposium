Academic Writing Guide

Marmara University, Department of Philosophy · Philposium '27

What Is Academic Writing?

Academic writing is evidence-based written work produced through scholarly methods, with the aim of generating knowledge, solving a problem or critically evaluating existing knowledge.

  • Purpose: It is written to identify an issue or solve a problem.
  • Objectivity: Claims should be as free as possible from the author’s subjective judgements.
  • Coherence: The arguments, evidence and references should form a coherent whole.
  • Verifiability: The references supporting the author’s claims must be verifiable.
  • Originality: The text should offer an independent, critical assessment of the problem it addresses. It should go beyond reporting existing views and establish meaningful connections between them.

Choosing a Topic and Defining a Research Problem

  • Accessible sources relevant to the chosen topic should be available.
  • Topics should be narrowed down. For example, choose a title such as “Traces of Philosophical Thought in Homer’s Texts” rather than “The Relationship between Myth and Philosophy in Ancient Greece”.
  • State the research question clearly in a single sentence.
  • Consider the topic’s contribution to the field: Why does it matter? What problem might it help resolve?
  • Check that the title matches the content. It should reflect the direction of the text and the problem it examines.

Literature Search and References

  • Consult primary sources directly where the research topic and method require it, and critically evaluate the secondary literature.
  • Secondary sources may include scholarly articles and studies of a philosopher or one of their works.
  • Examples of primary sources: Aristotle, Metaphysics; Plato, Symposium.
  • Example of a secondary source: Hasan Hüseyin Kargın, Platon’un Mutluluk Anlayışı [Plato’s Conception of Happiness], 2024.
  • Sources can be found through platforms such as Google Scholar, PhilPapers, JSTOR and DergiPark.
  • Identify keywords and search for them in both Turkish and English.
  • Take notes on the texts you read, recording the author, year, main idea and page number.
  • Every quotation used in the text must acknowledge the person or source quoted.

Sections of an Academic Text

  • Title: Choose a concise, clear title that reflects the content.
  • Abstract: State the purpose, method, findings and conclusion in 300–500 words. Include 3–5 keywords.
  • Introduction: Present the research problem, central thesis, significance of the study and overall direction of the argument.
  • Main body: Provide conceptual analysis, textual examination, development of arguments and an assessment of alternative interpretations and objections.
  • Conclusion: Set out the conclusions reached through the main arguments and the answer to the research question.
  • References: List all sources cited in the text.

Language and Style

  • Use clear, accessible and measured language. First- or third-person narration may be appropriate depending on the discipline’s writing conventions and the nature of the text. Avoid unnecessary use of the passive voice.
  • Avoid exaggerated, emotive or categorical statements, such as “definitely” or “everyone knows that”.
  • Avoid colloquial language and slang.
  • Define abbreviations at their first occurrence.
  • Use terminology consistently; do not use different terms for the same concept.
  • Use transitions to connect paragraphs and sections, such as “nevertheless”, “therefore” and “for this reason”.

Citations and Quotations

  • Always acknowledge the source of another person’s idea.
  • Use a single citation style throughout, such as APA, MLA, Chicago or IEEE. Check which style the institution or journal requires.
  • Place direct quotations in quotation marks and include the page number.
  • You may paraphrase in your own words, but you must still cite the source.
  • Every source cited in the text must appear in the reference list, and every entry in the reference list must be cited in the text.

Ethics and Plagiarism

  • Plagiarism is the use of another person’s text or ideas without acknowledging the source.
  • Fabricating or falsifying data violates research ethics.
  • For research involving human participants, obtain the ethics committee approvals required by the nature of the research and applicable regulations, and follow the principles of informed consent.
  • Disclose the use of tools such as artificial intelligence in accordance with the institution’s rules.

Revision and Final Checks

  • It can be helpful to set the draft aside for a while before rereading it.
  • Check the logical progression of the text: Are the question, method, findings and conclusion consistent with one another?
  • Correct any spelling, punctuation and formatting errors.
  • Check tables, figures and numbering.
  • Do the page layout, font and margins comply with the guidelines?
  • If possible, ask a peer or supervisor to read the text.
